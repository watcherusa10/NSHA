About NSHA

What NSHA does

The National Subsistence and Homesteading Association exists to make subsistence and homesteading lifestyles understandable, attainable, and durable for ordinary Americans. NSHA advocates for the people who grow, raise, preserve, and build for themselves, and works to remove the barriers of land, knowledge, and money that keep others from joining them.

Status: newly formed

NSHA is newly formed. The programs described on this page are in development, and anything marked “Planned” is not yet operating. This page sets out what the association intends to do and how to take part while it is built.

Progress on land and the model operation is reported in its own section.

Advocacy and policy

NSHA speaks for Subsisters before local, state, and federal bodies. Its aim is zoning, water, land-use, and food rules that let households provide for themselves without penalty.

Learn

What is subsistence? What is homesteading?

Two related ideas, one goal: meeting more of your household's needs with your own hands, land, and community.

What is subsistence?

Subsistence means producing a meaningful share of what your household needs to live: food, water, heat, and everyday goods. It does not mean producing all of it. A family that grows half its vegetables, keeps a few laying hens, and heats with wood it cut itself is practicing subsistence. So is a household that puts up a season of produce from a shared garden plot.

Subsistence is not the same as living “off the grid,” and it is not a rejection of modern life. Most people who practice subsistence hold jobs, use electricity, and shop at stores. It is also different from farming as a business, where the goal is to sell a crop at a profit. The goal of subsistence is to meet your own needs first, reduce what you must buy, and build a margin of safety for your household.

Subsistence Producing a meaningful share of what your household needs, rather than buying all of it.

What is homesteading?

Homesteading is the practice of organizing home life around subsistence. It brings together growing food, keeping animals, preserving the harvest, making and repairing things, and caring for land and water. Historically the word referred to settling and working a parcel of land. Today it describes a way of living more than a piece of property.

Homesteading happens at many scales. An apartment resident who grows herbs on a balcony, ferments vegetables, and mends clothing is homesteading. So is a suburban family that replaces a lawn with raised beds and a few fruit trees. So is a household on rural acreage with a barn, a woodlot, and livestock. The scale changes. The habits do not.

Homestead A home, at any scale, where the household produces food and meets other needs through its own labor.

Who Subsisters are

Who is a Subsister?

A Subsister is a member of the National Subsistence and Homesteading Association. Subsisters are at every stage, from those planting a first tomato to those stewarding working land. What unites them is a shared ethos: take responsibility for meeting your own needs, and stand ready to help others do the same. Self-reliance and mutual support are not opposites. A Subsister practices both.

Four principles

  1. Produce what you can. Buy what you must.
  2. Learn a skill, then teach it.
  3. Care for land and water as if you will hand them on.
  4. No household stands alone. The network is the safety net.

Common misconceptions

Myth

You need to own land to start.

Fact

Most subsistence skills can be learned in a rented apartment, a community garden plot, or a borrowed backyard.

Myth

Subsistence means going without.

Fact

People who practice subsistence use modern tools, hold jobs, and shop at stores; the aim is to depend on them less, not to abandon them.

Myth

Homesteading is a political statement.

Fact

People of every background and belief practice it, for reasons as plain as fresher food and lower bills.

Myth

It is a full-time job.

Fact

A productive household garden and a well-stocked pantry fit around a regular job and family life.

The public interest

Why it matters now

Four reasons a household's ability to provide for itself is a public concern.

Food security and resilience

Long supply chains are efficient but fragile. A household that can grow, store, and preserve part of its own food is less exposed when shelves are empty or prices climb.

Cost of living

Food, energy, and housing take a large share of household budgets. Every meal grown, every jar preserved, and every repair made at home is money that stays in the household.

Land access and the federal gap

The federal programs that once helped ordinary Americans settle and work land ended decades ago. Today's agricultural support is built mainly for commercial producers, so households that simply want to feed themselves have few doors to knock on. NSHA exists to open some.

Skills lost between generations

Within living memory, many households knew how to garden, can, butcher, mend, and build. Much of that knowledge was never written down, and it disappears when the people who hold it are gone.

The Subsister pathway

From curious to established, in five steps

Each step builds on the one before, and every step can be taken where you already live.

  1. 1

    Learn

    Understand the basics before you spend money on tools or land.

    • Reading a seed packet and a soil test
    • Finding your growing zone (the climate band printed on seed packets) and average frost dates
    • Keeping a simple garden journal
  2. 2

    Grow something

    Start small, bring in a real harvest, and learn from what fails.

    • Container herbs and salad greens
    • A single raised bed
    • Composting kitchen scraps
  3. 3

    Preserve and store

    Carry a harvest past its season so it feeds you through the year.

    • Water-bath canning of high-acid foods
    • Freezing and dehydrating
    • Setting up a cool, dark storage space
  4. 4

    Expand and network

    Add capacity and connect with people who can help.

    • Keeping a small flock of laying hens
    • Joining or forming a local Subsister node
    • Trading surplus and labor with neighbors
  5. 5

    Steward and mentor

    Manage land for the long term and pass your knowledge on.

    • Cover cropping or rotational grazing (moving livestock between pastures so each can recover)
    • Hosting an apprentice
    • Navigating land purchase, leases, and conservation easements

Glossary

Fourteen terms to know

Words you will see on this site and hear at NSHA events.

Subsistence
Producing a meaningful share of what your household needs, rather than buying all of it.
Homestead
A home, at any scale, where the household produces food and meets other needs through its own labor.
Subsister
A member of the National Subsistence and Homesteading Association, committed to self-reliance and mutual support.
Node
A small, local, self-organizing group of Subsisters (households, farms, or shared resources such as a seed library) that meets regularly and trades skills, tools, seed, and labor.
Chapter
The nodes of one region, working together. A chapter organizes regional events and carries the region's voice within the association.
Sliding scale
Dues set within a published range according to what a household can pay, so that cost is not the reason anyone stays out.
Cover crop
A crop planted to protect and improve soil between harvests rather than to eat or sell.
Root cellar
A cool, dark, humid storage space, often below ground, that keeps vegetables and fruit fresh for months without electricity.
Mutual aid network
A voluntary group whose members share labor, tools, knowledge, and surplus so that no household faces hardship alone.
Apprenticeship
A structured period of learning by working alongside an experienced practitioner on a functioning homestead.
Cost-share
A program that pays part of the cost of an approved project, such as a fence, a well, or a soil practice, while the landowner pays the rest.
Conservation easement
A voluntary legal agreement that permanently limits how a piece of land may be developed. The owner keeps the land and may receive payment or a tax benefit in return.
Water rights
The legal right to take and use water from a stream, well, or rainfall. The rules differ widely from state to state.
Right-to-farm law
A state or local law that protects ordinary farming activity from certain nuisance complaints and restrictions. What is protected varies by state.

The network

A decentralized subsistence support network

A decentralized network has no headquarters that everything depends on. It is made of many small, local groups, called nodes, that operate on their own and cooperate with one another. Each node knows its own soil, climate, laws, and neighbors. The nodes of one region together form a chapter, which organizes regional events and carries the region's voice within the association. Skills pass directly from one Subsister to the next. Seed, tools, and labor move between households without waiting on permission from anyone far away.

Because no single node holds everything, no single failure can stop the whole. If one group loses a meeting place or a leader moves away, the others carry on and help it rebuild. NSHA's role is coordination, not control. NSHA will set shared standards, keep the directory of nodes, and connect nodes to funding and one another, and it plans to provide insurance and legal guidance as the association matures. The work itself belongs to the nodes.

Each node is a household, farm, mentor, or shared resource. Darker nodes are anchor sites: farms and households with room to host apprentices. Nodes support one another directly. There is no headquarters that can fail.

How a node works

  1. 1

    Gather

    A small group of Subsisters in one area agrees to meet on a regular schedule, in person or by call.

  2. 2

    Inventory

    The node lists what its members have and what they need: skills, tools, seed, land, storage, labor, and time.

  3. 3

    Exchange

    Members trade, lend, teach, and work alongside one another according to the inventory, using simple agreements the node sets for itself.

  4. 4

    Connect

    The node registers in the NSHA directory, reports what it is learning, and draws on other nodes and the association when it needs more than it has.

Land and apprenticeship

Land and the model operation

NSHA plans to acquire property and operate a working subsistence homestead on it. The operation will produce food and materials for the people who work it, and it will also serve as a school. Apprentices will live and work on site for a season under experienced Subsisters. Visitors will be able to see raised beds, orchards, livestock, a root cellar, water systems, and energy systems in daily use. The goal is a place that proves the model works and sends trained Subsisters home to start their own.

Funding and grants

Funding and grants

Land, tools, livestock, and time all cost money, and the lack of it is one of the most common reasons a household does not start.

Public programs exist that can help, but they are scattered across agencies, written in technical language, and rarely explained to the people who need them most. NSHA treats funding as a core program, not an afterthought. NSHA will pursue grants as an organization, help individual Subsisters find and apply for what already exists, and raise donations so that the work does not depend on any single source.

Grants NSHA pursues and passes through

NSHA will apply for foundation, agricultural, conservation, and community development grants at the organizational level. A portion of funds raised is planned to pass through to regional nodes and to individual Subsister projects through a simple application process. Details will be published as funding is secured.

Programs to know

Named in general terms only. Eligibility and terms are set by each agency; follow the agency links for current details.

  • The USDA Farm Service Agency administers loan programs for farm ownership and operating costs, including options intended for beginning farmers. fsa.usda.gov
  • The USDA Natural Resources Conservation Service runs conservation programs, such as the Environmental Quality Incentives Program, that can support soil, water, and habitat practices on working land. nrcs.usda.gov
  • The Beginning Farmer and Rancher Development Program, administered through USDA, funds organizations that provide education, training, and technical assistance to new farmers. Individuals benefit by taking part in funded programs rather than applying directly. usda.gov
  • Grants.gov is the central listing of federal grant opportunities. grants.gov
  • Many states and counties also run cost-share programs, which pay part of a project’s cost, and other small-farm programs. Ask your local node what applies where you live.

NSHA provides information and assistance only. NSHA does not administer federal programs. Eligibility and terms are set by each agency, and NSHA does not guarantee eligibility for, or award of, any grant, loan, or program.
